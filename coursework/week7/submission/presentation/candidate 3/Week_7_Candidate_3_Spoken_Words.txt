This project asks a practical question: can an explainable model help people notice potentially urgent email without pretending that software should make the final decision? I will show the evidence gathered, what that evidence establishes today, where reliability breaks down, and what should happen next.

The operational problem is straightforward. Important messages can be buried in a high-volume inbox, but urgency is not just a keyword. It can depend on deadlines, requests, relationships, and context. A useful triage tool should surface messages for attention and show why the model produced its score. The boundary matters just as much as the prediction. False negatives can hide urgent work, while false positives can overwhelm the reviewer. For that reason, this project treats the model as decision support. The system scores the message and provides an explanation, but a person remains responsible for the routing decision.

The planned Week 8 demonstration will make that boundary visible. A reviewer will enter a synthetic or privacy-screened message and receive an urgency score, classifications at the two frozen thresholds, and a short list of features that supported or opposed the score. Those contributions explain the fitted linear calculation; they are not causal explanations and they do not prove that a message is urgent. The same result will state that human review is required, the disposition is revise, and neither shadow testing nor production deployment is approved. The application is designed not to echo the submitted subject or body into its saved validation results. In practical terms, the interface will expose evidence for review without automating the decision.

The evidence begins with the Enron email corpus, which contains five hundred seventeen thousand, four hundred one source records. The capstone evaluation uses a much smaller reviewed sample of one hundred ninety-nine messages: thirty urgent and one hundred sixty-nine nonurgent. One hundred fifty-nine messages form the fixed training partition, and forty form a preliminary holdout. That sample supports a controlled academic evaluation, not broad claims about every inbox or production prevalence. The raw corpus and detailed review material stay outside the repository, while privacy-screened labels, metrics, notebooks, and decision records provide an auditable evidence trail.

The project advanced through controlled checkpoints. First, the baseline established a reproducible T F I D F and logistic-regression reference. Next, model selection and explanation work used only the training partition. For Week 6, nested cross-validation separated selection from evaluation inside those one hundred fifty-nine training messages. Each outer fold acted as a temporary evaluation set, while the inner folds selected the threshold without seeing that outer fold's labels. The outer results were then combined for error analysis and the model decision. The preliminary holdout was examined only after the training-data decision was frozen, but earlier project work had already influenced it. It is therefore descriptive evidence, not a new untouched final test.

Optimization improved the training evidence, but the gains were modest. Mean cross-validated urgent F one increased from six point seven percent to eleven point four percent, urgent recall doubled from four percent to eight percent, and the selected T F I D F feature count decreased by twenty-nine percent. These results showed that the workflow could tune and reproduce a candidate. They did not establish a reliable classifier. At the frozen zero point four five threshold, the preliminary holdout identified all six urgent messages but also labeled all thirty-four nonurgent messages urgent. At zero point five zero, the same holdout found two of six urgent messages, missed four, and created one false positive. Together, those results reinforced the need to examine threshold behavior rather than treating recall alone as success.

Threshold testing exposed the central reliability problem. At zero point four five, urgent recall reached one hundred percent, but every one of the one hundred fifty-nine training messages was labeled urgent. That created one hundred thirty-five false positives and removed the value of prioritization. At zero point five zero, there were no false positives, but urgent recall fell to eight point three percent. The model found only two of twenty-four urgent messages and missed twenty-two. Another warning is that one hundred fifty-seven probabilities fell between the two thresholds. A change of only zero point zero five therefore moved the system from almost no urgent predictions to all urgent predictions. Neither threshold provides a defensible operating point.

Week 7 software validation was evaluated separately from predictive reliability. All thirteen automated tests passed. They covered the visible research-use disclosure, valid and invalid inputs, safe behavior when the model is unavailable, notebook execution, metric traceability, privacy-sensitive columns, and preservation of the revise decision. A separate validator ran three synthetic cases twenty times each, for sixty total inferences, and produced identical scores within each case. Five Gate A checks passed: artifact boundary, deterministic scoring, non-retention in the result record, threshold visibility, and bounded explanations. This evidence establishes software and interface readiness for the planned Week 8 demonstration. It does not substitute for labeled predictive testing.

The final disposition is revise. Gate A passed as an engineering-readiness check for the planned Week 8 demonstration. Gate B failed because neither tested threshold met the combined recall, precision, F one, review-burden, stability, calibration, and untouched-evaluation requirements. Human review remains required, and shadow testing and production deployment remain unapproved. This conclusion is not a failed project outcome. It is the reliability finding produced by the testing process. The work demonstrates a transparent pipeline, exposes the failure mode, and prevents a weak operating point from being overstated.

My recommendation is to demonstrate the validated research workflow in Week 8 while keeping the model decision unchanged. The next predictive experiment should add reviewed examples, reserve a genuinely untouched test set, use related-message grouping where needed, assess calibration, and review false negatives and a reproducible sample of false positives. The same privacy, reproducibility, human-review, and approval controls should remain in place. Only a separately versioned candidate that passes the predictive gates should be considered for a bounded, supervised shadow-test recommendation. Even that step would require an explicit human approval record; it would not create automatic production authorization.
